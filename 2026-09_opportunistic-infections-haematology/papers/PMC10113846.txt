PMC Copyright notice PMCID: PMC10113846 PMID: 37089309 Abstract Background Hepatosplenic candidiasis is a rare but severe complication in immunocompromised patients undergoing chemotherapy. Antifungal agents are widely accepted as the first choices for therapy. However, resistance to or side-effect of antifungal agents may comxpromise its efficiency. Splenectomy has also been rarely performed as treatment for this disease. Methods We present two cases of splenectomy for treating hepatosplenic candidiasis after failure of the initial drug therapy. Literatures on splenectomy as treatment for hepatosplenic candidiasis were searched in Pubmed and summarized. Results Two leukemia patients developed hepatosplenic candidiasis after received chemotherapy for their primary diseases. Various antifungal agents including amphotericin B were all demonstrated failure to cure fever and the Candida abscesses due to resistance or intractable side-effect. Laparoscopic splenectomy were finally performed and resolved the candidiasis successfully. A total of 12 splenectomy cases for treating hepatosplenic candidiasis had been previously reported in literature. All the cases showed either resistance or unimproved to initial antifungal therapies. Splenectomy provided salvage therapeutic value in all cases. Conclusion Splenectomy has therapeutic effect and may change the traditional concept in most surgeons. The present study may expand an alternative strategy in clinical practice guideline for the management of hepatosplenic candidiasis. Keywords: Hepatosplenic candidiasis, Candida , Infectious disease, Antifungal therapy, Splenectomy 1. Introduction Hepatosplenic candidiasis (HSC) is a very rare disease, also known as the most frequent form of chronic disseminated candidiasis due to Candida species infection that mainly involves the liver and spleen. It typically occurs in immunocompromised patients particularly those with profound and prolonged neutropenia after long-lasting chemotherapy, often seen when treating acute hematological malignancies [ 1 , 2 ]. The pathophysiology of this disease has been better understood in the past decades. However, despite the wide use of new antifungal agents as prophylaxis or as preemptive therapy, the incidence of HSC has not been dramatically decreased [ 2 , 3 ]. The break out of HSC in patients remains a problem of morbidity and mortality. The diagnosis for HSC mainly depends on clinical, radiological and direct or indirect microbiological evidences. Typical symptom is persisting fever without response to broad-spectrum antibiotics, especially after a previously afebrile neutropenia course and should prompt suspension for HSC. Lesions detected in imaging procedures such as ultrasonography, computer tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides probable diagnosis. Nevertheless, fungal cultures from blood and tissues are often negative, making the proof of diagnosis elusive. According to the criteria of the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC), diagnosis can be defined as proven, probable or possible [ 4 ]. It is now widely accepted that antifungal therapy is the first choice when treating HSC. The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) recommends continuous drug therapy until lesions resolved on repeat imaging, which usually takes several months [ 5 ]. No surgical practice was discussed in clinical consensus or guidelines. However, not all patients may have everlasting response to the drug treatment. Intrinsic or acquired resistance to antifungal agents has been proposed to be a major problem in management of Candida infection, leading to a poor prognosis [ 6 ]. Alternatively, splenectomy has been rarely performed as salvage therapeutic strategy for the treatment of HSC(7–11). Many surgeons may dispute its feasibility because only removal of the spleen could be helpless or even detrimental. Here we reported two cases of HSC who underwent splenectomy as salvage treatment after failure of the initial drug therapy. Written informed consent was obtained from both patients. Literatures on splenectomy for HSC were screened in Pubmed. Only 12 cases have been reported in 5 papers to date. After comprehensive review, we discussed the diagnosis and treatment especially the feasibility of splenectomy for this rare disease. This study was approved by the Committee of Ethics in Biomedical Research of Zhejiang University. 2. Case 1 A 54-year female was admitted to the department of Hematology in November 2021 for fatigue and leukocytosis. The initial blood test showed severe leukocytosis (total 72.76*109/L, including neutrophils 7.03*109/L, lymphocytes 20.5*109/L, monocytes 20.48*109/L), anemia (hemoglobin 90 g/L), and thrombocytopenia (58*109/L). Bone marrow aspiration revealed diagnosis of acute myelocytic leukemia (AML-M5b). Therefore remission-induction chemotherapy with HAA protocol (Homoharringtonine 3 mg qd + cytosine arabinoside 75 mg q12h, 7 days for 1 cycle) was started (day 0). Complete remission was soon achieved but complicated with severe neutropenia (the lowest neutrophils &lt;0.02*109/L). From day 3, broad-spectrum antibiotics were used for infection prophylaxis as the patient presented once fever. She regained afebrile immediately and persisted prolonged pancytopenia (23 days) even with several time treatments of granulocyte colony stimulating factor and component transfusion. The first febrile episode began on day 14 and voriconazole (200 mg, q12h) was given as empirical antifungal therapy. However, fever persisted with daily vesperal peak of 39 °C. Repeated hemocultures for fungus and bacteria were all negative. Metagenomics next generation sequencing (mNGS) of blood was then performed on day 26, by which Candida tropicalis DNA was detected. Antifungal agent was subsequently switched to amphotericin B (AmB) at a dose of 40 mg per day, which resulted in an afebrile period of 10 days. However, she developed intractable hypokalemia that was considered as the side effect of AmB. Voriconazole (200 mg, q12h) or fluconazole (400 mg, qd) in combination of micafungin (150 mg, qd) were consecutively selected as substitute therapy. A second febrile episode was then started. CT scan for the abdomen and thorax showed numerous low-density lesions in the spleen, and few within the liver and kidneys ( Fig. 1 A–C), consistent of abscesses. Fever persisted and repeat CT scan 2 weeks later found lesions slightly enlarged, demonstrating the failure of antifungal therapy. Fig. 1. Laparoscopic splenectomy was performed after multidisciplinary team consultation. Multiple granuloma resembling small abscesses were visualized in the resected spleen ( Fig. 2 A). Microscopic histopathology of the resected spleen revealed branching hyphae in the necrotic tissue background by special staining such as PAS staining, Gomori methenaine silver staining and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assay, consistent with Candida infection ( Fig. 2 B–E). Tissue culture for fungus was negative. The fever was immediately resolved during the postoperative days. She continued oral fluconazole therapy after discharge. One month later, she received a second chemo-cycle and remained in complete remission of AML. Case 2 A 32-year female was referred to remission induction chemotherapy with a VICLP (vincristine, idarubicin, cyclophosphamide, L-asparaginasum) protocol in August 2015 because of B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) diagnosis. Oral posaconazole (200 mg tid) was given for antifungal prophylaxis from day 3. A consecutive febrile episode with neutropenia started on day 8. Empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics were used, but she persisted high fever with vomiting and diarrhea. The antifungal agent was then replaced by voriconazole (200 mg, q12h) but showed ineffective. Thoracic CT scan on day 14 documented a large nodule with air crescent sign in the right lung, which strongly suggested fungal pulmonary infection. Meanwhile, blood cultures at three different days all reported the isolation of pan-azole resistant Candida tropicalis. The antifungal agent was then switched to AmB at a dose of 1 mg/kg/day and 5-flurocytosine (2.5g, q12h). However, scattered subcutaneous small abscesses started to spread throughout the body. Cultures of the drainage fluid and necrotic tissue from these abscesses both revealed similar pan-azole resistant Candida tropicalis as that from blood cultures. Abdominal CT scan showed massive small low-density lesions in the spleen and few scattered in the liver and kidneys, consistent with the diagnosis of HSC. Micafungin (150 mg, qd) was added to enhance the antifungal treatment and the subcutaneous abscesses recovered gradually after drainage but fever persisted. After 3 months, repeat CT scan showed an aggravation of the multiple organ abscesses especially in the spleen. Given no improvement in symptoms and imaging, laparoscopic splenectomy was performed after multidisciplinary team consultation. Dense tiny abscesses were noted in the entire resected spleen and microscopic histological examination confirmed the characteristic of Candida infection. The patient recovered well after surgery with no fever. Antifungal therapy with micafungin was maintained for one and half a year. She was in complete remission from ALL with no further evidence of candidiasis at 4-year follow-up. 4. Discussion Candida species are opportunistic pathogens that can be naturally found in the mouth and gastrointestinal tract of healthy individuals. In immunocompromised patients especially those received chemotherapy for acute leukemia, the risk for Candida infection increases and sometimes is life threatening [ 12 , 13 ]. Invasive candidiasis may disseminate to target organs through bloodstream. Translocation of pathogens to the liver and spleen causes HSC: a manifestation of chronic disseminated candidiasis. It has been hypothesized that disruption of the gastrointestinal mucosal barrier following chemotherapy and then local invasion allowed by prolonged neutropenia might be the possible pathogenesis of HSC [ 2 , 14 ]. However，a proven diagnosis of HSC is difficult to establish. Most of the clinical diagnosis for HSC were probable or possible. According to the EORTC's criteria, a proven candidiasis requires evidence from at least one of the following criterions: a tissue biopsy, a specimen culture or fungal DNA detection [ 4 ]. First, detection of Candida in blood is difficult because fungal cultures are generally negative in HSC. Only about 20% patients with HSC had positive blood culture [ 2 ]. A potential explanation for the low blood culture yield may be that the hematogenous dissemination of Candida limits to the portal vein system in HSC. Early antifungal prophylaxis may also contribute to decreasing the detective ratio. Furthermore, fungal isolation is less often in tissue cultures obtained from biopsy. In a retrospective study, tissue cultures were all negative in the HSC patients who underwent liver biopsy whereas blood culture only resulted in 12.5% positivity [ 15 ]. As non-cultural diagnostic techniques, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and mNGS have been recently validated for fungal detection [ 16 , 17 ]. In the updated consensus of EORTC, fungal nucleic acid amplification techniques were recommended in pathogen detection [ 4 ]. mNGS is a recent revolutionary technology that can simultaneously and independently sequence thousands to billions of DNA fragments. It allows for an unbiased approach to the detection of pathogens including fungus [ 18 ]. When organisms are fastidious or slow growing or have limited recovery from routine cultures, mNGS may provide high sensitivity of detection. In our first case, we applied mNGS and successfully detected Candida after failures in several blood cultures. Imaging evaluations are essential and highly recommended in the diagnosis of HSC, especially when the microbiological diagnosis is not established [ 1 ]. Ultrasound, CT and MRI can reveal multiple characteristic lesions. Ultrasound can be manipulated at bedside. Abdominal CT scan is more sensitive and has been widely used in evaluating HSC. Typical CT features are multiple hypodense nodular lesions in target organs with “bull's-eye” enhancement when performed with contrast. MRI is most accurate and sensitive for lesion detection in the liver and spleen. Both CT and MRI are often used for monitoring treatment response as well. Recently, PET/CT scan has been reported to have benefit for evaluating HSC. Uptake of 18F-FDG in the inflammatory lesions provides diagnostic value. It can reveal small lesions that are unapparent on the CT or MRI [ 19 ]. However, FDG also accumulates in neoplastic cells. In the setting of HSC in hematologic malignancies, sometimes it is not easy to differentiate neoplastic and inflammatory lesions. Thus, the role of PET/CT in evaluating HSC needs to be further studied. In the present study, we reviewed literatures that concerned splenectomy for treating HSC ( Table 1 ). A total of 12 cases were reported in 5 papers [ [7] , [8] , [9] , [10] , [11] ]. Together with our two cases, imaging procedures detected lesions in spleen of all cases whereas liver and kidney of some cases, providing the probable diagnosis of HSC. Only 4 patients were positive in blood culture. Though 6 patients were reported positive in pharynx, urine or stool cultures, these were usually considered as natural sites of Candida colonization. One patient had positive tissue cultures for both spleen and liver. The tissue cultures of our cases and others were all negative. However, pathological examination of the resected spleen all showed typical histological signs that were consistent with Candida infection. It was clear that visible fungal elements under direct microscopic or histopathological examination contributed to a proven diagnosis of HSC. To date, antifungal therapy is the cornerstone of treatment for disseminated candidiasis. With the advent of various antifungal agents such as active azoles, safer polyenes, and novel echinocandins, clinicians now have multiple choices [ 20 ]. Current treatment strategies for HSC mainly focused on the selection among various antifungal agents. The recent guideline for the management of candidiasis updated by the IDSA recommended AmB or an echinocandin (micafungin, caspofungin, or anidulafungin) as initial therapy. Therapy should be lasted for several weeks and followed by oral fluconazole if the isolate was not fluconazole-resistant, until lesions resolved on repeat imaging [ 21 ]. However, drug toxicity problems and high costs of some agents may preclude the long-lasting use in some patients. Furthermore, the prolonged treatment may lead to response failure or drug resistance. In one study, 8.2% of HSC patients had persistent residual lesions beyond one year even with various antifungal treatments [ 22 ]. Any intrinsic or acquired resistance or intolerance to toxicity of antifungal agents ultimately results in treatment failure. Alternatively, splenectomy might provide therapeutic effect. It had been rarely performed as salvage therapy on HSC. A total of 12 cases had been reported. We presented two cases of our own ( Table 1 ). All patients showed either resistance or unimproved to initial antifungal therapies, thus prompting to splenectomy as salvage treatment. Among them, most had complete recovery outcome. Only one case sustained fungal sepsis leading to patient death soon. But this patient underwent simultaneous colectomy. Such an expanded surgery on this critical ill patient might deteriorate the postoperative conditions. Although 3 other patients had death outcome, they all died of Candida unrelated diseases such as tumor relapse or aspergillosis. The candidiasis was controlled well after splenectomy in all other cases. These data suggested that splenectomy provided successful treatment for HSC patients when drug therapy failed. Splenectomy for treating infectious disease is controversial. The spleen is the largest lymphoid organ and plays important roles in human immune defense. It has been well known that splenectomy may predispose an increased risk for infection, in particular, overwhelming post-splenectomy infection [ 23 ]. A matched study also showed that previous splenectomy was one of risk factors associated with Candida bloodstream infection [ 24 ]. Therefore, it is not surprising that most surgeons may hesitate to perform splenectomy for patients of candidiasis. In traditional concept, one may also believe that removal of spleen might be helpless since Candida abscesses still remain in the other organs such as liver and kidney. The risk of breakout infection of the residual lesions after splenectomy is worrisome. The present study demonstrated the successful treatment of splenectomy in HSC patients. It is noteworthy that this clinical strategy had experimental evidence. Recent studies demonstrated that splenectomy could provide protective effects against polymicrobial sepsis by reversing immune suppression in animal models [ [25] , [26] , [27] ]. Therefore, we hypothesized that translocation of Candida through portal vein system resulted in fungal colonization in the spleen as an outcome of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome in immunocompromised patients, which might subsequently act as a source for Candida dissemination. Removal of the spleen may not only decrease the pathogen load but also eliminate the disseminating source, thus contributing to cure of HSC. However, the potential mechanisms need to be further studied. There are other limitations of this study. First, this is a descriptive study of case report and literature review. Only very few analogous cases could be recruited as splenectomy was seldomly performed in such rare disease. Larger size studies are needed to strengthen the study conclusions. Secondly, not all the selected patients are suitable for splenectomy because some of them may be critically ill after episodes of treatment for the primary disease and antifungal drug failure or severe drug side effects. Operative risks should be assessed before the procedure. In conclusion, HSC is a rare disease that manifests chronic disseminated candidiasis. Typical symptoms and abnormalities on imaging evaluations prompt probable diagnosis of HSC. The low detective rate yielded in routine cultures of body specimens makes the proven diagnosis difficult. Antifungal therapy is the first choice when treating HSC. However, prolonged treatment may lead to response failure or drug resistance. The present study demonstrated that splenectomy can increase the diagnostic value and provide therapeutic effect when drug therapy failed. This may change the traditional concept in most surgeons and expand an alternative strategy in the clinical practice guideline for the management of HSC.
