== ARTICLE TITLE ==
Role of therapeutic plasma exchange in paraquat poisoning: Our experience

== ABSTRACT ==
Paraquat poisoning is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in many parts of Asia. It can lead to multiorgan dysfunction. It is actively concentrated in lung tissues leading to pulmonary fibrosis. The case fatality is very high in this herbicide due to the lack of effective treatment options. Here, we present our experience of a young female with a history of paraquat poisoning presented with acute kidney injury with acute respiratory distress syndrome managed symptomatically and using therapeutic plasma exchange as a treatment modality.

== Introduction ==

Paraquat (1,1’-Dimethyl-4,4’-bipyridinium dichloride) is a toxic herbicide used for agricultural purposes globally and in India for its low cost and easy availability.[1] Paraquat poisoning is associated with suicidal intention or accidental exposure which results in fatal toxicity. The mortality rate of paraquat poisoning is extremely high, ranging from 33% to 92%, mainly due to multiple organ damage and pulmonary fibrosis and the most common cause of death is respiratory failure.[2] It is rapidly distributed to high-perfused organs such as the liver, kidney, and lungs with a volume of distribution of 1.2–1.6 L/kg−1.[3] Till date, there is no standard treatment for paraquat poising.[45] In the majority of cases, the cause of death is pulmonary fibrosis.[6] A wide range of extracorporeal procedures are employed for the decontamination of poisonous substances during emergency care.[7] Here, we present our experience in the treatment of paraquat poisoning with TPE and hemodialysis.

== Case Report ==

A 29-year-old female presented in a local hospital emergency with an alleged history of ingestion of gramashru (Paraquat dichloride 24%). She was a known case of schizophrenia on antipsychotics for 6 years. At admission, her vitals were BP-113/72 mmHg, PR- 72/min, RR-26/min, Temp-afebrile, Spo2- 97% on room air. Immediately, Fuller’s earth was used to minimize adsorption. One session of hemodialysis was done at a local hospital on the day of ingestion of poison. The patient was managed conservatively with IV antibiotics, steroids, and other supportive treatments. The patient was referred to our hospital for further management on day 4. Her vitals at the time of presentation were BP-110/70 mmHg, PR- 105/min, RR-26/min, Temp-Afebrile, Spo2- 88% on room air. On general examination, the patient was conscious, restless, and using inappropriate words. Mucosal lesions were present on the mouth and tongue. The patient appeared sick, was intubated, and put on vasopressor support. On respiratory examination bilateral crepitations were present. The patient was thoroughly evaluated. Echocardiography was normal which ruled out myocardial involvement. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) chest was done which was suggestive of pulmonary fibrosis with acute respiratory distress syndrome. Laboratory investigation revealed Hb–10.9 mg/dl, TLC-9.63 103/mm3, low platelet count 100 × 103/mm3. Renal function test showed acute kidney injury (AKI) and electrolyte imbalance. Liver function test showed raised total bilirubin level and transaminitis. In view of deteriorating renal and liver function tests, TPE was done. An informed written consent was obtained. One and a half plasma volume exchange was done with 100% replacement with FFP on the Spectra Optia apheresis system (Lakewood, Colorado 80215, USA) through the right femoral catheter. Anticoagulation was achieved with acid citrate dextrose. In all procedures, intravenous calcium replacement was given prophylactically to prevent hypocalcemia due to citrate-related toxicity. No major complications occurred during or after TPE procedures. Two sessions of plasma exchange and hemodialysis were done alternatively [Table 1]. N-acetylcysteine infusion was given. The patient’s renal and liver parameters gradually improved [Table 2]. She had good diuresis along with improvement in pulmonary function and was extubated and gradually shifted to non-invasive ventilation and then nonrebreather breathing mask. The patient was managed with intravenous antibiotics, steroids, and other supportive treatments. The patient gradually improved. However, on day 30, the patient desaturated and was intubated. Repeat HRCT showed significant progression of pulmonary fibrosis and basal consolidation [Figure 1a and b]. The patient’s condition worsened with respiratory failure, sepsis, and shock requiring vasopressor support. In spite of all the measures patient developed asystole and could not be revived.

== Discussion ==

Paraquat is one of the most frequently used herbicides, precisely in developing countries, including India.[8] In Asia-Pacific countries, self-poisoning through oral intake of paraquat is the predominant cause of morbidity and mortality. At the cellular level, the toxic effect of paraquat is mediated by its capacity to generate reactive oxygen-free radicals through redox cycling and hamper the mitochondrial electron transport chain.[5] In addition, it causes an alteration in the cellular redox state and leads to mitochondrial damage, a reduction in antioxidants, lipid peroxidation, and apoptosis.[9] The absorption of paraquat rapidly occurs through inhalation, ingestion, and broken skin.[3] It affects organs with high blood flow and oxygen tension, such as the lungs, heart, kidneys, and liver.[10] Paraquat is highly concentrated in the lungs through an energy-derived process accompanied by an amino acid pump.[10] Paraquat has a limited metabolism, and it is excreted unchanged in the urine with a mean half-life of 84 h.[10]

The management strategy is based on the individual’s clinical features, and it depends on the amount of poison ingested and the timeline since the exposure.[4] Various treatment modalities include activated charcoal or Fuller’s earth, hemodialysis, or hemoperfusion.[45] However, all the treatments are not effective for patients with severe poisoning and poor prognosis.

Paraquat shows rapid but partial absorption from the gastrointestinal tract after intoxication and faster distribution in various tissues. The peak tissue level reaches 6 h after ingestion, with increased with elevated concentration in the lung, kidney, liver, and muscle tissues.[3] The primary route of elimination is through renal excretion, and in cases of acute poisoning, renal function is significantly impaired, resulting in a notably delayed elimination process.[3] Significant paraquat poisoning is indicated by AKI, which is caused by paraquat-induced acute tubular necrosis or volume depletion.[11] In the present case, the patient had an increase in urea and creatinine levels after consuming the substance.

Intrinsic antioxidants are mostly found in the liver and are crucial for detoxification and enzymatic metabolism. Consequently, the liver is more susceptible to damage caused by reactive oxygen species. According to earlier research, paraquat intoxication causes an immediate liver injury that is marked by histological alterations and a sustained rise of liver aminotransferases.[5] Our patient experienced acute liver injury, with elevated bilirubin level and SGOT, SGPT level.

Many extracorporeal techniques for toxic material removal have been used in emergency care to improve detoxification and early recovery in intoxicated patients. Choosing the effective extracorporeal elimination technique depends on the characteristics of the toxicant and the accessibility of the suitable technique.

Numerous case studies support TPE’s efficacy in various poisoning scenarios.[1213] Kalabalikis et al., reported a case of paraquat poisoning in a family, where three children along with the mother underwent extracorporeal removal of plasma paraquat, three children recovered fully but the mother died. According to this study, the extracorporeal techniques had an effect on paraquat removal.[12] Kuwahara et al. reported a study of 5 patients of paraquat poisoning, three were treated with TPE alone, and other two were treated with combination therapy. According to this study, TPE had an effect on the removal of paraquat and it is more effective when used in combination therapy.[13] In another study done by A. Hariharan et al., 19 patients of paraquat poisoning were treated with therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE), 15 responded to the treatment and 4 failed to respond as their renal function test remained elevated. They concluded that TPE had an effect in the clinical course and that the outcome mainly depends upon the time of initiation of TPE.[14] As per the ASFA category, TPE in paraquat poisoning falls under category III 2C.[15] Our case report indicates that TPE significantly aids in paraquat removal from the bloodstream. To maximize its effectiveness, TPE should be initiated within a few hours of exposure. Delays in treatment can markedly reduce its benefits and may worsen outcomes, potentially leading to pulmonary fibrosis.

Various case reports have demonstrated that TPE is efficacious in 50%–85% of people who ingested with poisonous substances.[81416] This deviation might be due to variation in the initiation of procedure as a result of late presentation and deviation in the standard protocol for frequency of cycles.

== Conclusion ==

TPE plays a significant role in managing paraquat toxicity. The patient exhibited significant improvement following a 1.5-volume plasma exchange, which can be attributed to the elimination of toxins. Nevertheless, the patient’s condition worsened, indicating that in cases of severe systemic paraquat poisoning, even with prompt and intense therapy in emergency care, the patient’s prognosis remains unimproved.
