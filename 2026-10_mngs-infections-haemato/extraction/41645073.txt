Abstract Background Bacterial and fungal pulmonary infections (BFPI) are common in hematological patients and pose significant diagnostic challenges. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) is valuable for diagnosing BFPI. However, for hematological patients with limited access to lower respiratory tract samples (LRTS), the clinical value of blood-mNGS compared to LRTS-mNGS requires further investigation. Methods A retrospective analysis was conducted on 160 cases with suspected pneumonia who underwent both blood-mNGS and LRTS-mNGS within one week. Diagnostic performance and impacts on antimicrobial adjustments were evaluated using clinical composite diagnosis (CCD) as the reference. Results Compared to CCD, LRTS-mNGS showed significantly higher positive percent agreement (PPA) than blood-mNGS [93.7% (119/127) vs. 34.6% (44/127),  P  < 0.001], with negative percent agreements (NPA) of 87.5% (21/24) and 91.7% (22/24), respectively. Blood-mNGS showed higher PPA in neutropenic than non-neutropenic patients [56.8% (21/37) vs. 25.6% (23/90),  P  = 0.001], with unique fungal detection advantages, identifying additional fungi in 7 cases: Mucorales (3),  Aspergillus  spp. (2), both Mucorales and  Aspergillus  spp. (1), and  Pneumocystis  spp. (1). Resistance genes were detected only by LRTS-mNGS. mNGS positively influenced antimicrobial adjustments in 54.3% (69/127) of cases, particularly for pathogens with low empirical coverage, such as  Legionella  spp. (0.0%, 0/7),  Pneumocystis  spp. (52.4%, 11/21), and Mucorales (55.6%, 5/9). Blood-mNGS detected these pathogens at rates of 71.4% (5/7), 23.8% (5/21), and 77.8% (7/9), respectively. Conclusion LRTS-mNGS outperformed blood-mNGS in diagnostic performance and resistance detection. Blood-mNGS identified pathogens in one-third of BFPI cases, with value for certain fungal infections, especially in hematologic patients with limited LRTS access. Trial registration Not applicable. This study is a retrospective analysis and does not require clinical trial registration. Background Bacterial and fungal pulmonary infections (BFPI) remain leading causes of morbidity and mortality in patients with hematological disorders, particularly following chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) [ 1 – 3 ]. These infections are characterized by a wide variety of pathogens, including atypical organisms such as  Mycobacteria  and  Legionella , which require special culture conditions and extended times [ 4 ,  5 ], and fungal pathogens like Mucorales, which show low detection rates in conventional microbiological tests (CMT) [ 6 ]. Additionally, the incidence of polymicrobial infections is notably high in this population, further complicating the diagnostic process [ 7 ,  8 ]. Therefore, the early and accurate identification of causative pathogens is crucial for effective treatment. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has demonstrated high sensitivity, broad pathogen coverage, and a short turnaround time, making it valuable for diagnosing pulmonary infections in hematologic patients [ 9 – 11 ]. Zhang et al. and Wang et al. both reported that bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) mNGS detected pathogens in approximately 90% of hematologic patients, a rate higher than that of CMT [ 9 ,  12 ]. Shen et al. reported that early BALF mNGS could improve survival in allogeneic-HSCT (allo-HSCT) patients with pulmonary complications [ 13 ]. Our recent studies indicate that blood-mNGS can contribute to the etiologic diagnosis of pulmonary infections, as nucleic acids from pathogens localized in the infection site can enter the bloodstream and be detected [ 14 ,  15 ]. This method is particularly valuable for hematologic patients, especially when BALF collection is difficult due to thrombocytopenia or hypoxemia, and when infections like  Pneumocystis , often presenting with a dry cough, limit sputum production [ 16 – 18 ]. Therefore, it is crucial to determine whether blood samples can serve as an alternative to lower respiratory tract samples (LRTS) for mNGS detection of pulmonary infection pathogens, or at least identify specific scenarios where blood mNGS provides reliable diagnostic results. However, the clinical utility of blood-mNGS in this context remains unclear and requires further investigation. In this study, we retrospectively analyzed the mNGS results from blood and LRTS, including BALF and sputum, collected from patients with hematological disorders and suspected pulmonary infections. The aim was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of mNGS in this population. We hypothesized that an optimized mNGS assay would enhance the detection of causative pathogens in BFPI. Methods Study design and data collection We retrospectively analyzed 184 patients with suspected pulmonary infections who underwent mNGS testing of blood and LRTS (with the two samples collected within one week of each other) in the Institute of Hematology and Blood Diseases Hospital from March 2021 to March 2024 (Fig.  1 ). The decision to perform mNGS testing was made by clinicians based on clinical needs. After excluding 4 patients with incomplete medical records and 24 patients without CMT, a total of 156 patients (involving 160 cases and 320 mNGS tests) were finally enrolled. The LRTS included 90 (56.3%) BALF samples and 70 (43.8%) sputum samples. Diagnosis of pulmonary infections was made in accordance with the CDC/NHSN surveillance definitions [ 19 ]. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of the Institute of Hematology and Blood Diseases Hospital (IRB number: QTJC2023004-EC-1). The requirement for obtaining informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of our study. Fig. 1 Flow diagram of the study. Abbreviations: CMT, conventional microbiological tests; LRTS, lower respiratory tract samples; mNGS, metagenomic next-generation sequencing Conventional microbiological tests (CMT) In this study, CMT included culture, microscopic analysis, and various specific assays:  Aspergillus  galactomannan (GM), (1,3)-β-D-glucan (BDG),  Cryptococcus  antigen,  Mycobacterium tuberculosis  T-SPOT.TB, Xpert MTB/RIF, and viral polymerase chain reaction (PCR), as detailed in Supplementary Table  1 . Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) The mNGS workflow included nucleic acid extraction, library preparation, high-throughput sequencing, and bioinformatic analysis for microbial identification. Positive reporting criteria and detailed procedures have been described in our previously published work and are provided in the Supplementary Methods [ 14 ,  15 ]. Our study targets 7 key antimicrobial resistance (AMR) markers ( mecA ,  bla OXA−23 ,  bla OXA−48 ,  bla KPC ,  bla NDM ,  bla VIM , and  bla IMP ) across 5 clinically important pathogens ( Staphylococcus aureus ,  Acinetobacter baumannii ,  Klebsiella pneumoniae ,  Enterobacter cloacae , and  Escherichia coli ). These genes are critical contributors to AMR and were analyzed to understand their prevalence and clinical value. Although carbapenem resistance in  Pseudomonas aeruginosa  is common, it is primarily caused by membrane protein alterations and efflux pump overexpression, which are difficult to analyze with short-read mNGS and were not included in this study. Diagnostic evaluation and evaluation of clinical value A clinical expert panel conducted a retrospective evaluation of the etiology and clinical value of mNGS for each case. The panel consisted of one microbiologist, one radiologist, and two hematologists with expertise in infectious diseases, chaired by a senior hematologist. In cases of disagreement, the chair made the final decision. The clinical composite diagnosis (CCD) was determined based on all laboratory test results, including both mNGS and CMT, along with clinical presentation, imaging findings, antimicrobial adjustments, and patient outcomes. The enrolled cases, based on clinical evaluation, were classified into four categories: bacterial/fungal infections, viral infections, non-infectious cases, and cases with uncertain etiology. Since this study primarily focused on bacterial/fungal infections, pathogen distribution, resistance analysis, and clinical value assessment were conducted only in bacterial/fungal infection cases. Diagnostic performance analysis included all cases except those with uncertain etiology. The clinical value of mNGS was evaluated in two aspects: causative bacterial/fungal pathogen detection and antimicrobial adjustments. If the bacterial or fungal microorganisms detected by mNGS were evaluated as causative pathogen in the CCD, mNGS was considered to have a positive impact on diagnosis. Positive impacts on antimicrobial adjustments included the addition of antimicrobial agents to target detected causative pathogens, as well as the escalation or de-escalation of therapy based on mNGS results. Negative impacts referred to unnecessary antimicrobial use, while neutral impacts described cases where mNGS results did not influence antimicrobial management. Statistical analysis Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were described using the median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical data were presented as counts and percentages. Diagnostic performance was analyzed using several metrics, including positive percent agreement (PPA), negative percent agreement (NPA), positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and Cohen’s Kappa (Supplementary Methods). For PPA, NPA, PPV, and NPV, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were also calculated. Statistical comparisons and concordance were analyzed using the chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, Mann-Whitney U test, and Kappa statistics. A significance level of  P  < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results Basic characteristics A total of 160 cases (involving 156 patients) were enrolled in the study. After etiology assessment by the clinical expert panel, 127 cases were classified as bacterial/fungal infections, 20 as viral infections, 4 as non-infectious cases, and 9 as cases with uncertain etiology (Fig.  1 ). Cases with uncertain etiology were not included in further analysis, leaving 151 cases (147 patients, 99 males and 48 females) for detailed analysis. The median age of these patients was 45 years. The most common underlying conditions were acute myeloid leukemia (44.2%, 65/147), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (17.7%, 26/147), and lymphoma (15.0%, 22/147). Among the 151 cases, 30.5% (46/151) were in the neutropenic phase, and 80.8% (122/151) had been exposed to antimicrobial agents prior to undergoing mNGS testing (Table  1 ). Further analysis demonstrated that for both bacterial and fungal detection, the PPA of LRTS‑mNGS was significantly higher than that of blood mNGS (bacteria: 97.0% vs. 28.7%, fungi: 81.8% vs. 36.4%, all  P  < 0.001). Although LRTS-mNGS had a higher PPA than blood-mNGS and CMT, it failed to detect causative pathogens in some cases. Importantly, in 7 cases, pathogens were only detected by blood-mNGS, all of which were fungi. Among these, 4 involved single fungal infections (2 caused by  Aspergillus  spp, 1 caused by Mucorales, and 1 by  Pneumocystis  spp.), while 3 were mixed fungal infections in which blood-mNGS identified additional fungal pathogens (2 Mucorales, and 1 both Mucorales and  Aspergillus  spp.) not detected by other methods. In 10 cases, pathogens were detected only by CMT, comprising 5 bacterial pathogens [ Klebsiella oxytoca  (2),  Stenotrophomonas maltophilia ,  Acinetobacter baumannii , and  Burkholderia cepacia ] and 5 fungal pathogens [ Aspergillus  spp. (3), Mucorales, and  Sarocladium  spp.]. The numbers of genera detected by LRTS-mNGS, CTM and blood-mNGS were 189 (91.3%, 189/207), 81 (39.1%, 81/207), 58 (28.0%, 58/207), respectively. Among these, 21 cases had corresponding culture results, involving 23 bacterial strains (Supplementary Table  4 ). In these 23 strains, 100% concordance was observed between resistance genes and phenotypes: for the 8 resistant strains (methicillin-resistant  S. aureus  [MRSA], carbapenem-resistant  A. baumannii  [CRAB], and carbapenem-resistant  K. pneumoniae  [CRKP]), mNGS detected the corresponding resistance genes, whereas for the 15 susceptible strains (methicillin-susceptible  S. aureus , and carbapenem-susceptible Gram-negative bacilli), no target resistance genes were detected. LRTS-mNGS consistently detected the same pathogens along with their associated resistance genes (MRSA:  mecA ; CRAB:  bla NDM  in 2 cases and  bla OXA−23  in 2 cases; CRKP:  bla KPC ). Among the remaining 28 cases, although mNGS detected the target bacteria (including  S. aureus  with  mecA  in two cases), culture did not yield these pathogens. Notably, the corresponding blood-mNGS did not yield any resistance genes. Evaluation of the clinical value of mNGS Regarding the clinical value for causative pathogen diagnosis, among the 127 bacterial/fungal infection cases, LRTS-mNGS detected pulmonary pathogens in 93.7% (119/127) of cases, while blood-mNGS identified pathogens in 34.6% (44/127) of cases, and only 18.9% (24/127) of cases detecting all bacterial and fungal pathogens. However, blood-mNGS demonstrated advantages in detecting fungal pathogens, identifying pathogens in 4 cases of single fungal infections and providing additional diagnostic information in 3 cases of mixed fungal infections. These findings highlight the significant role of blood-mNGS in diagnosing fungal infections. In terms of clinical value for antimicrobial adjustment, mNGS provided a positive impact in 69 out of 127 cases (54.3%), while only 1 case (0.8%) showed a negative impact. The most common positive impact was the addition of antimicrobial agents to target previously uncovered pathogens based on mNGS results, accounting for 53.6% (37/69) of cases. Common pathogens with low empirical coverage included  Legionella  spp. (0.0%, 0/7),  Pneumocystis  spp. (52.4%, 11/21), and Mucorales (55.6%, 5/9) (Fig.  2 B). Among the cases infected with these pathogens, the proportion of cases with antimicrobial adjustments based on mNGS results was 100.0% (7/7), 61.9% (13/21), and 66.7% (6/9), respectively. Importantly,  Legionella  spp. and Mucorales were detected in blood-mNGS in 71.4% (5/7) and 77.8% (7/9) of cases, respectively, suggesting that blood-mNGS plays a significant role in guiding treatment for these infections. Discussion Hematological patients are at high risk for BFPI, but diagnosing these infections is challenging due to the difficulty in obtaining high-quality LRTS and the low sensitivity of CMT [ 9 ,  20 ]. This underscores the need for alternative approaches, such as blood-mNGS. Our study is the first to compare LRTS-mNGS and blood-mNGS in hematological patients with suspected pulmonary infections. We confirmed that LRTS-mNGS outperforms blood-mNGS, particularly in detecting pathogens and AMR. In contrast, blood-mNGS proved valuable for neutropenic patients and for identifying fungal infections. Additionally, we identified key bacterial and fungal pathogens commonly seen in hematological patients, providing crucial insights into the pathogen spectrum for more effective diagnosis and treatment. By comparing the diagnostic performance of different sample types, our study further emphasizes the importance and irreplaceable role of LRTS in pulmonary infection diagnosis. First, LRTS-mNGS showed much higher PPA than blood-mNGS (93.7% vs. 34.6%), indicating that samples directly from the infection site provide better detection performance. This finding is consistent with previous studies that showed superior performance of LRTS over blood samples in diagnosing pulmonary infections [ 21 ,  22 ]. Notably, when using CMT as the reference, the NPA of LRTS-mNGS was relatively low (27.3%). This is mainly because mNGS can detect pathogens (e.g.,  Legionella  spp.) that are frequently missed by CMT, which are then misclassified as false positives. However, when CCD was applied, the NPA of LRTS-mNGS improved substantially. This aligns with the findings of Niu et al., who reported that the specificity (NPA) of mNGS was 32% compared to CMT, but increased to 71% when clinical diagnosis was used as the reference [ 23 ]. Second, the higher microbial load in LRTS enhances the sensitivity of LRTS-mNGS in detecting AMR. In our study, among the 8 cases with detected drug-resistant pathogens, 2 were identified in blood samples, but no resistance genes were detected, further highlighting the superior capability of LRTS-mNGS in resistance gene detection. Finally, despite the advantages of LRTS-mNGS, CMT remains indispensable, particularly in diagnosing  Aspergillus  spp. infections. In our study, LRTS-mNGS did not outperform CMT in diagnosing  Aspergillus  spp. infections (PPA: 76.9% vs. 73.1%). This conclusion is similar to the findings of Yan Shi et al. [ 24 ], highlighting that a combination of multiple conventional diagnostic methods remains essential for the accurate diagnosis of aspergillosis. While blood-mNGS demonstrated lower overall performance than LRTS-mNGS, its value in specific clinical contexts is significant. Yuhui Chen et al. reported that plasma mNGS is particularly effective in neutropenic patients, highlighting its potential in this population [ 25 ]. Our findings align with this, blood-mNGS achieved higher PPA in neutropenic compared to non-neutropenic patients (56.8% vs. 25.6%). This may be due to a higher incidence of pathogens entering the bloodstream during neutropenia. Moreover, previous studies have shown that the sensitivity of blood-mNGS exceeds 50% for fungal infections [ 18 ,  26 – 28 ], while this performance may differ across different fungal genera. In our study, blood-mNGS demonstrated varying diagnostic performance, with a PPA of 77.8% for Mucorales, 42.3% for  Aspergillus  spp., and 23.8% for  Pneumocystis  spp. Notably, blood-mNGS was the most common positive method for Mucorales infections, likely due to their ability to invade the bloodstream [ 29 ,  30 ]. Additionally, blood-mNGS demonstrated a PPA exceeding 70% for  Legionella  infections, which are challenging to diagnose due to the need for specialized culture media and the limitations of urinary antigen and antibody tests, including false negatives and restricted detection to  Legionella pneumophila  [ 31 ,  32 ]. This study provides a comprehensive overview of the etiological spectrum of BFPI in hematological patients by combining blood and LRTS with conventional methods and mNGS technology. The results highlight the broad range of pathogens in this population. Approximately 30% of the cases in our study were caused by pathogens not covered by prior empirical antimicrobial therapy, particularly  Legionella  spp.,  Pneumocystis  spp., and Mucorales. Additionally, mNGS led to effective antimicrobial adjustments in more than half of the cases, facilitating timely and appropriate changes in antimicrobial therapy, particularly for infections caused by rare or difficult-to-identify pathogens, such as  Nocardia  spp. in immunocompromised patients [ 33 ,  34 ]. Impaired immune function in hematological patients, coupled with the widespread use of antifungal prophylaxis, has led to an increase in infections caused by rare fungi [ 35 ,  36 ], such as  Fusarium  spp.,  Lomentospora  spp., and  Trichoderma  spp. These findings further emphasize the diverse spectrum of pathogens affecting hematological patients. Moreover, while CMT typically detects a single pathogen, over 40% of the cases in our study were caused by more than one bacterial or fungal pathogen, emphasizing the importance of broad-spectrum testing. Building on our findings, this study underscores the practical value of mNGS in enhancing clinical workflows and its potential to improve diagnostic accuracy and treatment decisions. Although blood-mNGS has certain limitations in detecting pulmonary pathogens, it remains the preferred option in clinical practice due to the risks and challenges associated with obtaining LRTS in real-world settings. In such situations, blood-mNGS serves as a timely, non-invasive diagnostic tool, offering crucial support for early diagnosis and treatment decisions. While prior studies have evaluated the diagnostic value of mNGS using either blood or LRTS alone for detecting pulmonary infections in hematological patients [ 18 ,  26 ,  37 ], there has been a lack of research on the combined use of both samples for mNGS. Our study, based on a large sample size, validates the value of blood-mNGS for diagnosis of BFPI in hematological patients and clarifies the appropriate sample selection strategy for these patients. Given the high cost of mNGS, the benefits of parallel testing with both blood and LRTS for BFPI are limited. In cases of suspected pulmonary infection, LRTS should be prioritized for testing. However, if LRTS cannot be obtained, or if LRTS results are negative but there is a high clinical suspicion of fungal infection, blood-mNGS can serve as a supplementary method. Our study has several limitations. First, due to the difficulty in obtaining BALF samples, some of the LRTS in this study were sputum samples. Although all sputum samples were quality-checked and deemed acceptable, there remains the possibility of detecting commensal organisms rather than true pathogens. Second, due to the limited availability of molecular diagnostics in developing countries, the pathogens we identified were not confirmed by molecular testing. Finally, as our study is a single-center retrospective analysis, validation through larger sample sizes and multi-center studies is necessary to confirm and generalize our findings. Conclusions In summary, this study demonstrates the complementary roles of LRTS-mNGS and blood-mNGS in diagnosing BFPI in hematological patients. While LRTS-mNGS outperformed blood-mNGS in overall diagnostic accuracy and resistance gene detection, blood-mNGS proved particularly valuable in specific contexts, such as fungal infections and neutropenic patients. For diagnosing BFPI, LRTS-mNGS remain the preferred choice. These findings highlight the necessity of integrating both blood- and LRTS-based molecular diagnostics into clinical workflows to enhance diagnostic precision and inform targeted antimicrobial strategies, and ultimately improving outcomes for high-risk hematological patients.
