== ARTICLE TITLE ==
Acute tubular necrosis with secondary acute respiratory distress syndrome after organophosphate poisoning: A rare clinical case

== ABSTRACT ==

== Introduction ==

Organophosphate (OP) poisoning is a serious and potentially life-threatening condition caused by exposure to pesticides, herbicides, and nerve agents [1]. These compounds irreversibly inhibit acetylcholinesterase, leading to acetylcholine accumulation and overstimulation of muscarinic and nicotinic receptors [2]. Although the nervous system is primarily affected, multiple organ systems may also be involved. Renal involvement in OP poisoning is recognized, but acute kidney injury (AKI) is considered an uncommon and secondary complication [3]. A cohort study reported that individuals exposed to OP poisoning had a 6.17-fold higher risk of developing AKI compared to a control group [2].

Renal impairment in OP poisoning occurs through multiple mechanisms, including direct tubular toxicity, oxidative stress–induced cellular injury, hypoperfusion due to volume depletion, and secondary systemic effects such as rhabdomyolysis and metabolic disturbances [4]. Clinically, renal injury may be characterized by oliguria, anuria, or elevated serum creatinine and may progress to irreversible renal damage if not promptly recognized and managed [5]. Severe cases may require renal replacement therapy [6]. This case reported a rare presentation of intrinsic acute tubular necrosis (ATN), supported by a fractional excretion of sodium of 3 %, indicating direct nephrotoxicity rather than prerenal injury, which is seldom reported. The concurrent development of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) further distinguished this case, as renal and pulmonary complications are usually described separately. Prompt antidotal therapy, renal replacement, and ventilatory support can lead to full recovery, providing key insights into managing severe organophosphate toxicity in resource-limited environments.

== Case presentation ==

A 41-year-old female photographer was admitted to the emergency department (ED) approximately 3 hours after intentionally ingesting 48 % soluble liquid chlorpyrifos in a suicide attempt following marital conflict. The patient had been experiencing significant emotional stress related to ongoing domestic disputes and had no prior psychiatric history. She developed vomiting, abdominal pain, progressive shortness of breath, altered mental status, generalized weakness, and reduced urine output, which later progressed to generalized body swelling. Her condition deteriorated rapidly, and she exhibited signs of multi-organ involvement. The patient had no known medical history, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, or liver disease. She had no history of smoking, alcohol consumption, substance abuse, or occupational exposure to organophosphate pesticides. There was no family history of renal disease, psychiatric illness, or other chronic conditions.

On admission, she appeared acutely ill, confused, and dehydrated, with peripheral edema. Vital signs revealed hypotension (BP 101/68 mmHg), tachycardia (HR 102 beats/min), and tachypnea (RR 17 breaths/min), with a normal axillary temperature of 37.1 °C. Neurological examination demonstrated miosis and altered mental status. The patient was oliguric, with pale skin and generalized swelling. The Glasgow Coma Scale score was 8 (E2M3V3), indicating severe impairment of consciousness. Mild abdominal tenderness was noted, with no evidence of trauma.

Arterial blood gas analysis showed severe metabolic acidosis (pH 7.16, pCO2 38 mmHg, O2 saturation 87 %, HCO3− 17 mmol/L). Complete blood count was normal. Renal function tests demonstrated AKI, with serum creatinine 4.1 mg/dL and blood urea nitrogen 47 mg/dL. Electrolytes revealed serum potassium (4.9 mmol/L), mild hyponatremia (133 mmol/L), and mild hypocalcemia (7.8 mg/dL). Liver function tests were normal. Serum cholinesterase level was markedly reduced (3290 U/L), and toxicological screening confirmed chlorpyrifos. Urinalysis showed proteinuria, hematuria, low urine osmolality (295 mOsm/kg), elevated urine sodium (48 mmol/L), and a fractional excretion of sodium of 3 %, consistent with intrinsic ATN. Urine output remained oliguric, dark, and concentrated.

Chest radiography demonstrated pulmonary edema, and abdominal ultrasonography revealed bilaterally enlarged kidney. Non-contrast brain CT showed no intracranial abnormalities. Mild hepatic dysfunction was noted but did not progress. After approximately 5 hours of stabilization in the ED, the patient was transferred to the ICU. Atropine and pralidoxime therapy were initiated along with supportive care. Based on clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings, the final diagnosis was ARF due to intrinsic ATN secondary to chlorpyrifos poisoning, complicated by ARDS.

The patient received intravenous fluids (0.9 % saline, 1000 mL over 6 hours) to correct dehydration and electrolyte imbalances. Atropine was administered IV as a 0.6 mg loading dose, followed by 0.02 mg/kg/hr infusion, started within 4 hours and tapered over 72 hours. Pralidoxime was given IV, 30 mg/kg bolus, then 8 mg/kg/hr infusion for 48 hours, starting 2 hours after ICU admission, with an additional 20 mg/kg IV dose 24 hours later.

Oxygen therapy was initially provided at 4 L/min via nasal cannula, then increased to 10 L/min via Venturi mask. She received mechanical ventilation for ARDS for 60 hours for ARDS. Hemodialysis was initiated 12 hours after ICU admission and repeated for 3 sessions to manage AKI. Over 84 hours, renal function improved, urine output increased, respiratory distress resolved, and mental status improved. After five days in the ICU, she was transferred to the inpatient department and discharged after seven days with plan for psychiatric support and monthly ambulatory nephrology follow-up.

== Discussion ==

OP poisoning is a major public health concern, with approximately 3 million exposures annually and a fatality rate of up to 300,000 [4]. OP cause toxicity primarily through irreversible inhibition of acetylcholinesterase (AChE), leading to acetylcholine accumulation and overstimulation of muscarinic and nicotinic receptors [2]. Kidney involvement occurs in about 4 % of patients as a predictor of increased mortality in OP poisoning [2]. Most previous reports have focused on neurological symptoms and secondary systemic complications [3,7,8]. In contrast, this case highlighted a rare instance of intrinsic ATN resulting from direct tubular toxicity.

Previous case reports primarily involved men across various ages, with the youngest reported by Zafar et al. [4] at 23 years and the oldest by Rahimi Majd et al. [2] at 74 years, while one case involved female, such as a 17-year-old reported by Cavari et al. [8]. Most cases involved unknown doses or lower-concentration OP compound, including methidathion [7], dichlorvos [5,6], diazinon [2], or 40 % chlorpyrifos [8]. This case is notable for being a 41-year-old female who intentionally ingested 48 % soluble liquid chlorpyrifos in a suicide attempt, illustrating that high-concentration OP formulations can lead to severe multi-organ toxicity. In prior studies, AKI in OP poisoning was mostly secondary to hypotension, shock, rhabdomyolysis, or systemic complications, as reported by several authors [3,4,[7], [8], [9], [10]], with some cases showing acute cortical necrosis, reported by Kumar et al. [5], or severe recurrent AKI, reported by Veeranki et al. [6]. In contrast, this case demonstrated intrinsic ATN, confirmed by elevated creatinine, oliguria, proteinuria, hematuria, low urine osmolality, high urine sodium, and a fractional excretion of sodium >2 %. Renal hypoperfusion induced by dehydration, hypotension and bradycardia, have direct toxic effects on renal parenchyma, endothelial cell damage, activation of immune and inflammatory responses, formation of free radicals, convulsive seizure and muscular fasciculation related rhabdomyolysis are all contributed to the decline of renal function [2]. Unlike conventional cases where AKI arises secondarily from prerenal factors [2,3], this case highlighted a direct nephrotoxic mechanism involving mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation (Fig. 1), which has been underreported previously. Management of OP poisoning includes airway support, oxygen therapy, intravenous fluids, atropine, and pralidoxime [3,4,7]. In previous cases with secondary AKI, supportive care and standard anticholinergic therapy often sufficed [4,5,[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Similar to previous reports [2,4,5,7,8,11], in this case the patient was successfully managed with hemodialysis. This case also received mechanical ventilation, and intensive monitoring due to intrinsic ATN complicated by ARDS. Anticholinergic therapy reduced muscarinic symptoms, and pralidoxime reactivated AChE, but advanced renal pathology necessitated additional interventions. Unlike the case reported by Cavari et al. [8], this patient did not receive renal replacement therapy initially.

The prognosis of OP poisoning depends on the severity of exposure, and timeliness of treatment [6,7,12,13]. Renal impairement is usually reversible with toxin removal, as evident in this case, where the patient showed gradual improvement in renal function by the end of one week, consistent with Rahimi Majd et al. [2]. The majority of reported cases of OP poisoning-induced AKI had unfavorable outcomes despite receiving dialysis [3,6,8,9,11]. This was attributed to several toxicokinetic properties of OP, including rapid tissue distribution and high volume of distribution [9]. The patient reported by Agostini et al. was died at 60 days [10].

== Limitations of this study ==

This case report has several limitations. It described only a single patient, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Histopathological confirmation of intrinsic tubular necrosis was not performed, so the diagnosis was inferred from clinical and laboratory data. Long-term follow-up of renal function and potential late complications was incomplete. Exact toxicological quantification of chlorpyrifos in blood or urine was not available, limiting the ability to correlate toxin levels with clinical severity. Additionally, potential confounders, such as stress, subclinical pre-existing conditions, or concurrent medications, may have influenced the patient's outcomes but were not fully controlled.

== Conclusion ==

This case demonstrated intrinsic ATN following organophosphate poisoning, a complication rarely reported in the literature. It highlighted that renal injury in OP poisoning is not always prerenal and may result from direct nephrotoxicity. Additionally, the patient developed ARDS, illustrating the potential for severe multi-organ involvement. Clinicians should be aware of such concurrent complications and initiate early interventions, including antidotes, renal replacement therapy, oxygen therapy, mechanical ventilation, and supportive care. Early recognition of both direct tubular injury and ARDS is essential to improve outcomes and guide appropriate monitoring and follow-up.
