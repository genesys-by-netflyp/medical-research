== Background ==

Aluminum phosphide (ALP) is a highly toxic pesticide, which is widely used in agriculture. Ingestion leads to multiorgan dysfunction through phosphine-induced cellular toxicity. Most commonly reported causes of death are cardiovascular collapse, metabolic acidosis, and arrhythmias. There exists no distinct antidote, and attentive supportive care is the key here.

== Case presentation ==

We report a 28-year-old Bangladeshi male who has a history of a major depressive disorder and attempted suicide by taking a single tablet of ALP (approximately 3 g). The patient came in with hypotension, irregular pulse, vomiting, and severe metabolic acidosis. ECG showed atrial fibrillation. The patient was put on aggressive intravenous fluids, titrated noradrenaline infusion, oral N-acetylcysteine administered through nasogastric tube, intravenous hydrocortisone, sodium bicarbonate and the usual supportive care in the ICU. Although he became unstable at first, his condition improved over time; the arrhythmia resolved and the acidosis corrected; he was discharged on the sixth day with psychiatric follow-up.

== Conclusions ==

With prompt and effective supportive care, survival in severe cases of ALP poisoning can be achieved. Rapid hemodynamic stabilization, correction of metabolic derangements, and antioxidant therapy can help to improve the outcome. The case highlights the need to recognize it as soon as possible, closely monitor it, and implement preventive strategies to decrease ALP-associated fatalities.

== Introduction ==

Aluminum phosphide (ALP) or a rice tablet is a solid fumigant pesticide that is used to a large scale to protect stored grains by pests in agricultural areas or South Asia. It is a ubiquitous household and commercial pesticide due to its low price, high effectiveness and easy accessibility [1]. However, unfortunately, it has also become the cause of its popularity in intentional self-poisoning, which is a serious public health problem.

Globally, pesticide self-poisoning remains a major public health concern, with an estimated 258,234 deaths each year, accounting for approximately 30% of all suicides worldwide. When evidence-based corrections are applied to India’s likely underreported official data, the global estimate rises to 371,594 deaths, underscoring the substantial contribution of pesticide ingestion [2].

Evidence from multiple regional analyses underscores the extreme lethality of aluminium phosphide poisoning. Regional data consistently highlight the high lethality of aluminium phosphide poisoning, with a pooled mortality of 54% in India [3], rates reaching 72.5% in Egypt [4], and 27–40% in Iran [5], aligning with earlier reports showing 37–100% fatality, with a fatal dose for an adult ranging from 0.15 to 0.5 g, most deaths occurring within 24 h due to refractory shock and arrhythmias [1, 6, 7].

Upon contact with humidity or with hydrochloric acid in the stomach, ALP rapidly releases large quantities of hydrogen phosphine (PH3) gas through chemical reactions in the stomach or ambient environment. Phosphine is a powerful cytotoxic compound that blocks mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation by suppressing cytochrome c oxidase. cellular respiration is impaired, which causes a decrease in ATP production, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and apoptosis [1]. The most common target is the cardiovascular system, with most common presentations are refractory hypotension, myocarditis, arrhythmias, and cardiogenic shock [8]. The secondary hypoxia, oxidative stress and direct cell toxicity cause secondary effects on the kidneys, liver and lungs. Gastrointestinal toxicities are near-universal, and metabolic derangements, including severe metabolic acidosis and electrolyte imbalances, are common [9, 10].

Despite decades of research, no specific antidote has yet been developed for ALP poisoning. Therefore, the cornerstone of therapy remains early intensive supportive care, including hemodynamic stabilization, correction of metabolic disturbances, management of arrhythmia, and reduction of oxidative stress. Experimental regimens, including the use of N-acetylcysteine (NAC), magnesium sulfate, glucose-insulin-potassium (GIK) infusion and advanced forms of supportive (ECMO, IABP, CRRT) have been described [11–14], though robust evidence is limited and mostly anecdotal. Survival is uncommon, particularly in patients who present with profound shock and cardiac arrhythmias, and every reported case of a successful outcome counts toward clinical education.

In this case, we discuss a case of severe ALP poisoning in a young man with major depressive disorder history, who had cardiogenic shock, atrial fibrillation, and severe metabolic acidosis, but survived following intensive supportive care. This example brings to the fore the practical approaches in early recognition, monitoring, and treatment in the resource-limited environments, and can inform clinicians working with such life-threatening cases.

== Case description ==

A 28-year-old Bangladeshi male deliberately ingested one aluminum phosphide (ALP) tablet (approximately 3 g), which was fresh and dissolved in water prior to ingestion. The patient initially had nausea, recurrent vomiting, abdominal pain, and dizziness. The patient had a history of major depressive disorder for the past several years and had previously received irregular psychiatric follow-up. He had no known chronic medical illnesses such as diabetes, hypertension, renal disease, or cardiac disorders. There was no significant family history of psychiatric illness, sudden cardiac death, or genetic disorders. He was initially examined in a local hospital where he underwent gastric lavage prior to being transferred to our tertiary care facility. He presented to our tertiary care facility approximately 7 hours after ingestion. At the time of presentation, he was ill, irritable and anxious. Physical examination revealed a tachycardia with 104 beats per minute, BP 85/60 mmHg, oxygen saturation was 95 percent on room air, and the respiratory rate was 22 breaths per minute. His cardiac examination revealed an irregular rhythm and abdominal examination showed mild tenderness without guarding or rigidity. Neurological observation was not remarkable.

Blood gas analysis (intended arterial sampling) showed metabolic acidosis (pH 7.36, HCO₃⁻ 11.7 mmol/L, pCO2 21 mmHg, pO2 44 mmHg). The relatively low pO2 value, despite preserved oxygen saturation, suggests possible sampling variability, and oxygenation parameters should be interpreted with caution. Renal and hepatic function tests were within normal limit [Table 1]. Electrocardiogram (ECG) showed atrial fibrillation with a rapid ventricular rate. Based on the clear history of deliberate ALP ingestion, severe metabolic acidosis, hypotension, and atrial fibrillation on ECG, the primary diagnosis of Acute aluminum phosphide poisoning with cardiogenic shock and metabolic acidosis was made. Emergency treatment aimed at the stabilization of hemodynamics and the normalization of metabolic disorders. The patient was put on aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation using normal saline and Hartmann solution and titrated infusion of noradrenaline (initiated at 0.05 µg/kg/minutes) to sustain a mean arterial pressure of more than 65 mmHg. Intravenous hydrocortisone was administered in the setting of persistent hypotension despite adequate fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support, as part of management of refractory shock. Oral N-acetylcysteine was administered via a nasogastric tube as an adjunctive measure, as intravenous formulation was not available in our setting. Prior to administration, antiemetics and proton pump inhibitors were given to improve tolerability. Sodium bicarbonate was administered to treat severe metabolic acidosis. Atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response was initially managed conservatively due to hypotension and cardiogenic shock. After partial hemodynamic stabilization, rate control was initiated with a low-dose beta-blocker (bisoprolol), with careful monitoring for hemodynamic stability. Other supportive care included antiemetics, proton pump inhibitors, intensive care unit (ICU) close monitoring, bed rest and close fluid balance adjustment.

Despite ongoing therapy, the patient initially showed deterioration manifesting as persistent hypotension and worsening acidosis (pH 7.29, HCO3− 11.1 mmol/L on day two). The noradrenaline infusion was then titrated upward (0.5 µg/kg/min). Gradual improvement was seen over the following 48 hours with the ABG parameters returning to normal on day 3 (pH 7.44, HCO3− 23.9 mmol/L, pCO2 35.6 mmHg, pO2 49 mmHg). The atrial fibrillation gradually reverted to sinus rhythm by day four, coinciding with correction of metabolic abnormalities and overall clinical improvement. On day five, the patient was able to tolerate oral intake and her vital signs and laboratory parameters improved with time [Table 1]. The patient tolerated oral intake from day five, with steady improvement in vital signs and laboratory parameters. On the sixth day, he was hemodynamically stable, awake, and ambulatory. Psychiatric referral was made to optimize the management of underlying depressive disorder and he was discharged on oral therapy of proton pump inhibitor, antiemetic and antidepressant.

== Follow-up and outcomes ==

Three weeks after discharge, the patient was followed up. He complained of no cardiovascular or neurological issues, and reported complete recovery. This positive outcome underscores that early, intensive supportive care may enable survival in cases of severe ALP poisoning.

== Discussion ==

Aluminum phosphide toxicity is still among the most fatal toxicological emergencies. In severe cases, the main cause of early deaths is early cardiovascular collapse which appears as a severe shock, arrhythmias, and myocardial dysfunction. Electrolyte abnormalities and severe metabolic acidosis further aggravate cardiac dysfunction [10]. The toxicity of aluminum phosphide may be influenced by factors such as the freshness of the tablet, method of ingestion (e.g., dissolution in water), and time interval before presentation, which can affect the rate and extent of phosphine gas release and systemic absorption. Here, the fact that our patient, who arrived with cardiogenic shock, atrial fibrillation and severe acidosis, survived provides evidence of the paramount importance of early intensive supportive care and timely diagnosis.

The management remains purely supportive since there is no available antidote to date. The main interventions are aggressive intravenous fluid services, vasopressor to sustain perfusion, correction of acidosis with sodium bicarbonate, and management of arrhythmia with the use of Beta blockers or antiarrhythmic drugs as needed [15]. Antioxidant treatment, especially with N-acetylcysteine, can be used to counteract oxidative stress and has been found to have positive outcomes in several studies [12, 16–18].

The most promising adjunct treatment of aluminum phosphide poisoning is N-acetylcysteine (NAC), in part due to the fact that oxidative stress is a significant cause of cellular injury caused by phosphines. NAC, which is a precursor of glutathione and a strong free-radical scavenger, attenuates lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction. The advantage is supported by clinical evidence: a randomized trial found a lower mortality rate, as well as lower levels of serum malondialdehyde and a shorter hospital stay [11, 19]. Other Indian studies have documented better cardiovascular stability, more stable heart rates, increased systolic blood pressure, and reduced complications associated with arrhythmias and significant decreases in CK-MB and CPK levels in patients who received NAC [20, 21]. Although not all reports have found a difference in the survival time, cardioprotective and antioxidant effects have consistently been reported. These findings are also supported by experimental models, which show that the antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD, catalase, GST, GSH) was restored, hepatic and renal damage were reduced, and survival in ALP-poisoned animals was prolonged [22, 23]. Collectively, the available evidence suggests that NAC prevents oxidative and cardiac complications in ALP poisoning, which is supported by the positive outcome in our patient.

However, its use, particularly via the oral route, remains controversial, given concerns regarding gastrointestinal intolerance and the theoretical risk of enhanced phosphine gas release when dissolved in water. In the present case, oral NAC was administered via a nasogastric tube due to the unavailability of intravenous formulation, and only after several hours had elapsed since ingestion, by which time significant phosphine absorption had likely already occurred. The patient tolerated the therapy without apparent adverse effects; however, a causal relationship between NAC administration and clinical improvement cannot be established. Therefore, oral NAC should be considered a context-specific alternative in resource-limited settings, and its routine use cannot be recommended without further robust evidence.

Several case reports [24–26] have described survival in severe aluminum phosphide poisoning despite the presence of shock, arrhythmias, and severe metabolic acidosis. Although mortality remains high, especially in patients presenting with cardiovascular collapse, some reports have documented favorable outcomes with aggressive supportive care and adjunctive antioxidant therapy, including N-acetylcysteine. These cases highlight that survival is possible even in critically ill patients when early resuscitation and intensive care support are provided.

Hydrocortisone may be considered in patients with refractory shock; however, the supporting evidence remains largely anecdotal [27, 28]. In our case, it was administered in the context of persistent hypotension despite fluid and vasopressor therapy. In high-resource facilities, there are advanced modalities such as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) or intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) reported to increase survival in otherwise fatal cases [29, 30].

Although the patient in this report has survived without any renal or hepatic compromise, the outcomes are quite diverse depending on dose, time of presentation, underlying wellbeing, and presence of intensive care. Further studies are necessary to fidentify out specific antidotes, assess the effect of antioxidant and immunomodulatory therapies, and establish evidence-based protocols in resource-limited conditions. Early recognition and aggressive supportive care is the surest way of lowering mortality due to ALP poisoning until such strategies are adopted. This case highlights the challenges of managing severe poisoning in resource-limited settings, where clinicians may need to rely on pragmatic adaptations when standard therapies are unavailable.

This case has several notable features. First, the patient survived after presenting with cardiogenic shock, atrial fibrillation, and a high level of metabolic acidosis, which have a high mortality rate in the context of aluminum phosphide poisoning. Second, oral N-acetylcysteine was administered via a nasogastric tube as a pragmatic alternative in a resource-limited setting where intravenous formulation was unavailable. Third, the patient had a complete recovery without the implementation of the advanced circulatory support technologies like extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) and intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP).

== Limitations ==

This report has a number of limitations. Phosphine was not measured in the blood because of unavailability, which restricted association with severity. Echocardiography was not conducted to determine myocardial performance. The use of oral N-acetylcysteine represents a limitation, as its absorption and tolerability may be variable, and its efficacy in aluminum phosphide poisoning remains uncertain. Since it is a single-case report, the results might not be generalizable.

== Strengths ==

The present case report has a number of strengths. First, it describes survival following severe ALP poisoning, which is relatively rare given the high mortality associated with profound shock and arrhythmias. Second, it shows the possibility of a favorable outcome of timely supportive treatment such as hemodynamic stabilization, normalization of metabolic disturbances, and antioxidant therapy with N-acetylcysteine. Third, the detailed timeline, laboratory data, and management strategies provide valuable educational insight for clinicians, particularly in resource-limited settings. Lastly, the case reinforces current, though limited, research on the effectiveness of NAC in enhancing the outcomes in ALP poisoning.

== Conclusion ==

The case highlights that, with early and intensive supportive care, survival following severe poisoning by aluminum phosphide with cardiogenic shock, atrial fibrillation and metabolic acidosis is possible. Quick hemodynamic stabilization, normalization of metabolic imbalances, and antioxidant treatment played a role towards the positive outcome. Regulatory restrictions and public awareness are necessary to curb the number of deaths caused by ALP.

== Guidelines ==

The case report was prepared in accordance with CARE guidelines [31] for clinical case reporting.

== Learning points for clinicians ==

Physicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for ALP poisoning in regions where the compound is commonly used. Early recognition and ICU-based supportive management are crucial. N-acetylcysteine may have a potential role as an adjunctive antioxidant therapy; however, its optimal route and efficacy remain uncertain, and further studies are required.. Aggressive management of shock, arrhythmias, and metabolic derangements can improve survival. Public health measures, including limiting ALP access and addressing mental health needs, remain vital in preventing fatalities.

== Uniqueness of the case ==

This case is unique because it documents survival in a patient with severe aluminum phosphide poisoning complicated by profound cardiogenic shock, atrial fibrillation, and severe metabolic acidosis, conditions that are frequently fatal. The case underscores the practical application of early and aggressive supportive care, including intravenous fluids, vasopressor titration, sodium bicarbonate, hydrocortisone, and oral N-acetylcysteine administered via nasogastric tube when intravenous formulations were unavailable.
