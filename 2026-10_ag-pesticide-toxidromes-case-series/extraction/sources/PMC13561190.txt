== ARTICLE TITLE ==
Favorable outcome in paraquat poisoning with acute kidney injury in a resource-limited setting: a case report

== Introduction and importance: ==

Paraquat poisoning remains a major public health challenge in Asia, with a mortality rate of 60%–80%, and acute kidney injury being a common complication.

== Case presentation: ==

An 18-year-old female presented 6 hours after ingestion of approximately 5 mL of 24% (1.2 g) paraquat. Gastric lavage was performed within 30 minutes. She developed oral mucosal ulcers, dysphagia, and progressive AKI with serum creatinine rising from 1.0 mg/dL on the day of admission to 7.3 mg/dL by day 6 (eGFR declining from 83.7 mL/min/1.73m2 to 7.7 mL/min/1.73 m2). Chest radiography on day 3 showed prominent bronchovascular markings, and liver enzymes were significantly elevated. Despite indications for hemodialysis, it was not performed due to financial constraints. The patient was managed with intensive supportive care, including corticosteroids, N-acetylcysteine, antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors, and fluid resuscitation. Three-month follow-up showed no respiratory, gastrointestinal, or renal manifestations.

== Clinical discussion: ==

Favorable prognostic factors included young age, rapid gastric decontamination, a moderate ingested dose (26 mg/kg), relatively preserved initial renal function, and early, aggressive supportive therapy.

== Conclusion: ==

This case demonstrates that survival is possible in cases of moderate-dose paraquat poisoning with AKI, even without hemodialysis, when early supportive care is promptly initiated. While this single case cannot be generalized, it highlights the critical importance of rapid decontamination and aggressive supportive management in resource-limited settings where paraquat remains readily accessible, and it underscores the need for further research to develop affordable, evidence-based treatment protocols for paraquat poisoning in developing countries.

== Introduction ==

Paraquat (1,1′-dimethyl-4,4′-dipyridinium chloride), commonly referred to as methyl viologen, is widely employed globally as a quaternary ammonium-based herbicide for controlling broadleaf weeds, owing to its highly effective intracellular action in agricultural and horticultural applications[1]. As an organic heterocyclic pesticide compound, paraquat (PQ) poses severe toxicity risks to humans. The combination of its affordability and ready availability has led to frequent use in suicide attempts, creating a major public health challenge throughout Asia[2]. Fatality rates associated with PQ intoxication can reach as high as 60–80%, with acute pulmonary damage, advancing lung fibrosis, and acute kidney injury being the predominant causes of death[3]. The hallmark of PQ toxicity involves significant reactive oxygen species-mediated tissue damage, leading to acute multiple organ failure[4]. Despite the high mortality, there are no widely accepted treatment guidelines. The treatment approach ranges from purely supportive measures to comprehensive protocols incorporating immunosuppressive therapy, antioxidant agents, hemoperfusion, and hemodialysis[5]. Hemodialysis is commonly indicated when PQ poisoning is complicated by kidney injury. However, in resource-limited countries like Nepal, where dialysis facilities are not readily available and are unaffordable, there is a need for exploration of alternative strategies. The article is reported in line with the CARE guidelines. The article is compliant with the TITAN 2025 Guideline[6].

We report a case of PQ poisoning with acute kidney injury. What distinguishes this case from previously reported survivors is the combination of three co-occurring factors:[1] a moderate-to-high ingested dose (26 mg/kg, within the range typically associated with severe multi-organ dysfunction),[2] documented Stage 3 AKI by KDIGO creatinine criteria, and[3] survival without hemodialysis, which was indicated for severe metabolic acidosis with a high anion gap. While reports of survival at lower doses or without AKI exist[7], this constellation, particularly the severity of renal impairment managed without renal replacement therapy, has been infrequently documented.

References were identified through a non-systematic search of PubMed and Google Scholar using the terms “paraquat poisoning,” “paraquat toxicity,” “paraquat acute kidney injury,” “paraquat treatment,” and “paraquat resource-limited settings.” Additional references were identified from the citation lists of retrieved articles.

== Case presentation ==

An 18-year-old woman presented to the emergency department 6 hours after ingesting approximately 5 mL of 24% PQ, taken with suicidal intent. Thirty minutes after ingestion, she was taken to a nearby hospital, where gastric lavage was performed and activated charcoal was administered. She experienced 12 episodes of vomiting. On arrival at our center, she complained of sore throat, epigastric pain, and chest pain but denied difficulty breathing. She had no significant past medical history. On examination, she was alert, conscious, and oriented to time, place, and person. Her vital signs were blood pressure 130/80 mmHg, heart rate 109 beats per minute, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute, temperature 98°F, and oxygen saturation 96% on room air. Respiratory examination revealed no signs of distress, and both lungs were clear on auscultation. The remainder of the systemic examination was unremarkable. Acute kidney injury was diagnosed and staged according to KDIGO 2012 criteria. The rise in serum creatinine from 1.0 mg/dL to 7.3 mg/dL represents a 7.3-fold increase from baseline, meeting KDIGO stage 3 criteria (serum creatinine >3 times the baseline)[8]. The leukocyte count increased from a baseline of 8680/µL to a peak of 16 500/µL on day 5, showing a 90% increase above baseline, as shown in Figure 2. The electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia. The patient was admitted to the medical intensive care unit for close observation and further management. During hospitalization, she was treated with antibiotics (ceftriaxone 1 g IV twice daily), a proton pump inhibitor (pantoprazole 40 mg IV twice daily), steroids (dexamethasone 8 mg IV three times daily), N-acetylcysteine 1 g IV three times daily, intravenous fluids, analgesics, and antiemetics. Dexamethasone was initially planned for a 7-day course with subsequent dose tapering, but the patient was discharged on day 7. The treatment was guided by previous reports[5]. She was kept nil per oral NPO. Within the first 24 hours, she developed dysphagia along with ulcers on her tongue, lips, and buccal mucosa. On day 3, arterial blood gasABG analysis revealed severe metabolic acidosis with a high anion gap. The initial chest X-ray was normal. A repeat chest X-ray performed on day 3 showed prominent bronchovascular markings in the right middle zone and slight heterogeneity in the bilateral lower zones (Fig. 3). The differential diagnoses considered included acute gastroenteritis, other toxic ingestions, and intrinsic renal disease. However, the diagnosis of PQ poisoning was established based on[1] a clear and corroborated history of intentional ingestion of a known PQ-containing herbicide with suicidal intent[2], characteristic early mucosal injury (oral ulcers, dysphagia) occurring within 24 hours of ingestion[3], progressive multi-organ involvement in a temporal pattern consistent with PQ toxicity, and[4] absence of any prior renal, hepatic, or pulmonary disease.

Hemodialysis was planned for the management of AKI and its metabolic complications, specifically high anion gap metabolic acidosis, but due to financial difficulties and an uncertain outcome, the family discharged her against medical advice.

The patient was contacted via video calls at intervals of 1 week to 10 days until 3 months post-discharge. Her general condition appeared fair; she was well-oriented to time, place, and person; and she denied any history of cough, shortness of breath, chest pain, difficulty swallowing, fatigue, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, swelling of the face or limbs, palpitations, or effort intolerance during the video calls. Despite our ongoing counseling to visit the hospital for an in-person evaluation, including offering discounted investigations, she ultimately did not present to the hospital.

== Discussion ==

PQ, being a caustic compound, causes corrosive lesions of the mucosa along the gastrointestinal tract upon ingestion. These manifestations can range from mucosal lesions of the mouth and tongue, called the “Paraquat tongue,” to esophageal ulcerations resulting in pain and dysphagia. These symptoms can occur not only due to initial ingestion of the substance but also from re-exposure when the patient tries to vomit it out, whether induced or as a result of the poison itself[7]. PQ toxicity operates through two primary mechanisms. First, it triggers the production of superoxide anions, which subsequently generate more harmful reactive oxygen species, including hydrogen peroxide and hydroxyl radicals. Second, it causes oxidation of cellular NADPH, the cell’s primary source of reducing power, thereby disrupting essential NADPH-dependent metabolic processes[7,9]. PQ absorption is enhanced when the gastric or intestinal mucosa is damaged or when ingestion occurs on an empty stomach[9]. Approximately half of patients with PQ poisoning develop acute kidney injury[10]. PQ accumulation in renal tissue triggers acute kidney injury via oxidative stress from excessive reactive oxygen species generation. This presents clinically as impaired renal function, demonstrated by declining glomerular filtration rate, rising creatinine levels, and oliguria. Severe poisoning may cause acute tubular necrosis, culminating in acute renal failure[7]. In our patient, the initial serum creatinine and eGFR on the day of presentation were 1.0 mg/dL and 83.7 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively, which progressively worsened each day, and on Day 6, they were 7.3 mg/dL and 7.7 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively, consistent with the existing literature on the development of AKI after PQ ingestion. Re-entry of PQ into the system can also result in pulmonary and hepatic complications. The liver is the primary organ for metabolizing PQ and contains major sources of intrinsic antioxidants like glutathione. Thus, the liver becomes a major target in PQ poisoning, resulting in acute liver injury (ALI), which may manifest as jaundice, vomiting, metabolic disorders, and altered liver function tests such as elevated serum transaminases[7]. Although our patient did not develop ALI (normal INR, no features of hepatic encephalopathy), she had a significant rise in her transaminases (AST and ALT), which were 8 U/L and 16 U/L on the day of presentation, and they gradually increased to 242 U/L and 192 U/L on Day 6. The lungs concentrate PQ far more than plasma, often 6–10 times more, because of active uptake by alveolar cells, where it is retained even when blood levels start to decrease. PQ’s toxic effects on the lungs occur because lung cells contain high levels of polyamine transporters, particularly in alveolar type I and II cells as well as Clara cells, which facilitate PQ uptake into pulmonary tissue[11]. Progressive pulmonary fibrosis is an important late complication of PQ toxicity, which can cause death 2-3 weeks later due to hypoxia and respiratory failure[7].

Multiple prognostic factors have been identified for predicting outcomes in PQ poisoning, including the quantity of PQ consumed, the patient’s age, the time interval from ingestion to hospital arrival, early clinical presentations such as vomiting or loss of consciousness, and the rapidity with which complications develop. These variables collectively help clinicians assess mortality risk and guide treatment decisions[7]. Our patient weighed 45 kg and ingested 1.2 g of PQ, which is equivalent to 26 mg/kg. Studies have shown that an amount between 20 and 40 mg/kg of PQ is generally associated with a very poor prognosis, with severe mucosal damage followed by MODS in many patients, while amounts higher than 40 mg/kg are considered fatal[7]. Despite the poor prognosis suggested by the ingested PQ amount, our patient survived. A meta-analysis conducted by Huang et al. showed an association between blood creatinine levels and clinical prognosis in patients with PQ intoxication, demonstrating that patients with elevated blood creatinine had a significantly increased risk of mortality. This meta-analysis compared serum creatinine values within 24 hours of ingestion among survivors and non-survivors, showing 0.61–1.05 mg/dL in survivors and 1.01–3.13 mg/dL in non-survivors[2]. This finding favors our patient’s likelihood of survival and places her in the survivor category according to the meta-analysis, as her initial serum creatinine was 1 mg/dL.

Since no specific antidote has been clinically validated, treatment focuses on supportive care measures. The therapeutic approach encompasses several strategies: administering antioxidants such as vitamins C and E to combat oxidative damage in pulmonary tissues, implementing high-dose corticosteroid therapy combined with cyclophosphamide to inhibit the development of lung fibrosis, removing PQ from the bloodstream through dialysis, and decontaminating the gastrointestinal tract[12]. The outcome depends on the severity of the poisoning and the time taken to seek medical help. The high mortality rates are due to the toxicity of the compound itself and to the lack of a specific antidote. Better outcomes in PQ intoxication are associated with several factors: younger patient age, non-oral routes of exposure (dermal or respiratory), lower ingested doses, and less severe initial clinical manifestations, including reduced leukocyte counts, less pronounced acid-base disturbances, and minimal impairment of renal, hepatic, and pancreatic function upon initial evaluation[13]. Zhou et al. also showed that younger age (mean 30 years), shorter time to hospitalization (mean 8 hours), and lower creatinine, ALT, bilirubin, leukocyte, and neutrophil counts at admission were significantly associated with favorable outcomes[14]; all of these were present in our patient. Resource-limited healthcare settings face unique challenges in PQ poisoning management. In Nepal, hemodialysis costs approximately NPR 2500 (USD 19) per session, representing 2.5 days of average rural income[15].

Our case demonstrates that when dialysis is unavailable, aggressive supportive care within the first few hours may still achieve favorable outcomes in selected patients. Possible reasons for survival in our patient include the relatively small ingested dose, immediate gastric lavage within 30 minutes, a lesser degree of leukocytosis, no hepatic failure, and early aggressive supportive care. Although indications for hemodialysis, such as severe metabolic acidosis with a high anion gap, were present, the patient survived without undergoing hemodialysis. A meta-analysis by Eizadi-Mood et al showed no survival benefit of hemodialysis in PQ poisoning due to its large volume of distribution and extensive tissue binding[16]. The Government of Nepal provides free dialysis services to patients with chronic kidney disease[17]. This policy should also extend to cases of acute poisoning when individuals from lower socio-economic classes cannot afford dialysis.

The patient’s decision to leave against medical advice (LAMA) on Day 6 raises important ethical considerations. Continued hospitalization was recommended due to worsening acute kidney injury and the risk of delayed pulmonary complications. However, the family opted for discharge primarily because of financial constraints and uncertainty about the prognosis. In resource-limited settings, LAMA for economic reasons is a common challenge and differs from refusal of care based on informed choice. In such situations, clinicians must balance respect for patient autonomy with the responsibility to ensure that decisions are truly informed and not driven by financial pressure. To support continuity of care, we maintained communication through telephone and video follow-up after discharge.

There are several limitations to our study. Post-discharge follow-up was conducted exclusively via video calls at intervals of 1–2 weeks over three months. No in-person clinical examination, laboratory reassessment (including repeat serum creatinine or liver enzymes), or chest imaging were performed. This limits our ability to objectively confirm complete organ recovery and represents a significant constraint on the conclusions that can be drawn from this case. Despite repeated counseling and an offer of discounted investigations, the patient declined to attend an in-person evaluation. The favorable clinical impression at three months is therefore based on patient-reported symptoms and general appearance on video review, and is not confirmed by objective data.

== Conclusion ==

This case demonstrates that survival following moderate-dose PQ ingestion (26 mg/kg), complicated by acute kidney injury, is possible. The favorable outcome in the patient may be attributed to rapid gastric decontamination within 30 minutes of ingestion, the patient’s young age, prompt initiation of supportive therapy, including corticosteroids and N-acetylcysteine, and relatively preserved initial renal function. While financial constraints prevented access to hemodialysis, a common challenge in resource-limited settings such as Nepal, early aggressive supportive care was associated with survival in this instance of moderate PQ poisoning. However, this single case cannot be generalized without larger systematic studies, and the importance of dialysis when available remains paramount, given the persistently high mortality rate of 60-80% in PQ poisoning. This case highlights the urgent need to develop affordable treatment protocols for resource-constrained settings where PQ remains readily accessible, to establish early prognostic markers to guide resource allocation, and to enhance access to dialysis services in developing countries.
